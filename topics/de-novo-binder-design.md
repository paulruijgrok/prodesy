# De novo binder design

A chapter on designing a protein that binds a chosen target, starting from the target's structure — or, latterly, from its sequence alone — without immunization and without library selection. The subject is worth a chapter rather than a concept page because it is the part of protein design where the field's methods, its oracles and its wet-lab discipline all meet, and because it has been *solved* repeatedly in a specific sense: each generation of method deleted one thing the designer previously had to supply. First the **binding mode** went — motif grafting required a natural interaction to copy, and hotspot-conditioned generation removed that requirement. Then the **scaffold** went — the designer supplies a target and a patch, and the backbone is invented. Then the **chemistry** went — all-atom models dropped the requirement that the target be a protein at all. One specification has survived every generation: somebody still has to decide **where on the target to bind**, and that decision is still largely made by hand.

Running underneath that progression is a second story, and it is the one this chapter treats as its main argument. As generation got easier, the binding constraint moved downstream — from generation, to *in silico* filtering, to experimental throughput — and the field's reported success rates became **mutually incommensurable** along the way. A yeast-display SC50 hit rate, a biolayer-interferometry single-point response, an AlphaFold2 self-consistency pass rate and a fraction of designs reaching nanomolar K_D are four different numbers with four different denominators, and papers quote whichever their assay produced. This chapter states each figure in its own units and declines to average them. The most direct evidence that this is not pedantry comes from outside the published literature: [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] measured six binder pipelines on one target class inside one project and found six different denominators, with a dedicated comparability ledger explaining why no two of them can be placed side by side.

## 1. What de novo binder design means, and what it replaces

The object of the exercise is a protein that binds a chosen target with useful affinity and specificity, designed from structural information about the target rather than discovered by screening. [[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl]] characterises the state this capability had reached by 2023: design of high-affinity binders from target structure alone had become possible by Rosetta-based motif grafting, but success rates were low enough that large numbers of candidates had to be screened experimentally. The therapeutic and diagnostic motivation is stated across the sources — [[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]] calls high-affinity binder design a grand challenge with numerous therapeutic applications, and [[vasquez-torres-2023-helical-peptide-binders]] carries its designs through to serum diagnostics.

Three things must still be specified, and it is worth separating them because the chapter's history is the history of the first two being automated:

- **The target**, as a structure, a sequence, or — for the all-atom methods of §7 — a small molecule or nucleic acid.
- **The epitope**: where on the target to bind. In practice this is a list of hotspot residues, and §2 explains why that is a physically coherent instruction. No method in this chapter chooses the epitope for the designer, with one partial exception: [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]] uses MaSIF-site to *predict* which surface patches have a high propensity to be engaged by a protein, which is epitope nomination rather than epitope selection.
- **The modality**: a 50–130-residue miniprotein, a peptide, a nanobody, an antibody. Until recently this was a property of the method; [[stark-2025-boltzgen]] is the first ingested source for which it is an input, and [[bennett-2026-de-novo-antibodies]] the first for which an **antibody framework** is a conditioning input with the rigid-body placement left to be designed.

The chapter excludes affinity maturation of existing binders, interface *prediction* as an end in itself, and the interfaces internal to symmetric homo-oligomers, which are treated in [[symmetric-oligomer-design]].

**What this replaces is the weakest-sourced claim in the chapter.** Binder discovery has conventionally proceeded by immunization or by display-library selection, and the de novo literature positions itself against that. No ingested source states this history directly; the closest available support is indirect — [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]] compares a designed SARS-CoV-2 binder's potency to the clinical antibody imdevimab and notes that its buried interface area of 1,452 Å² is smaller than the ~2,071 ± 456 Å² typical of antibodies, and [[stark-2025-boltzgen]] motivates nanobody design by that modality's developability advantages. A primer on display or antibody engineering is flagged in §14.

## 2. The biophysics: what an interface is, and where its energy lives

Every method after §3 is told where to bind by naming **hotspot residues**, so the chapter needs to say what a hotspot is. [[halperin-2004-interface-hot-spots]] gives the operational definition: a hot spot is a residue whose mutation to alanine causes a significant drop in binding affinity, measured as the change in free energy of binding, with the working threshold set at **ΔΔG ≥ 1.9 kcal/mol**. That is a mutagenesis definition, not a geometric one, and the paper's contribution is to connect the two.

Its dataset is two-sided, which is the point. Structurally conserved residues were drawn from a non-redundant interface set that had grown from 1,629 interfaces in 351 clusters in 1996 to **21,686 interfaces in 3,799 clusters**; experimental hot spots came from the Alanine Scanning Energetics database, screened for complexes where *both* partners had been alanine-scanned and the pair had been crystallised together, yielding **8 complexes, 429 alanine mutations and 53 hot spots** ([[halperin-2004-interface-hot-spots]]).

The physical result is that hot spots are more densely packed than the rest of the interface, and the packing tracks the energetics. Hot spots have on average **4.2 neighbours across the interface** against **2.3** for non-hot-spot interface residues, a difference significant at p = 0.01 and 2.9 standard deviations across 53 hot spots and 551 non-hot-spot residues. The occupied volume in a residue's vicinity is **69.2 Å³ (SD 42.5) around hot spots** against **38.1 Å³ (SD 17.1)** elsewhere, significant at the 0.99 confidence level. And the occupied volume **correlates with ΔΔG at r² = 0.94** ([[halperin-2004-interface-hot-spots]]). The effect is not an artefact of residue size: amino-acid volume correlates with hot-spot propensity at r² = 0.01, and with neighbour count at r² = 0.03.

The synthesising claim in that paper is that a protein–protein interface "consists of regions of high and low packing density, with the hot spots organized in the former", and that the high local packing density in interfaces is **reminiscent of protein cores** ([[halperin-2004-interface-hot-spots]]). An interface is therefore not a uniform patch of surface: it has a core-like dense region carrying most of the binding energy and a looser periphery. Hot spots also **couple across** the interface more often than a random distribution predicts, with expected couple counts of 2.6 (SD 4.0) under a purely random null and 5.4 (SD 6.0) under a null that additionally conditions on neighbour count — so the elevated neighbour count accounts for much, though not all, of the coupling.

The wiki's reading is that this is what makes hotspot conditioning a coherent instruction rather than a convenient fiction. Telling a generative model to contact a named set of target residues is telling it to build into the region where binding energy is concentrated and where packing is densest — and because the property is regional rather than pointwise, *soft* conditioning that merely biases toward those residues can still work, which is how every method in §5 and §7 in fact implements it ([[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]]).

Chemistry is the second axis, and it is not the same as packing. [[halperin-2004-interface-hot-spots]] finds strong Cys–Cys conservation across interfaces; glycine preferentially coupled with aromatics, polar and charged residues and with small hydrophobics but disfavoured beside large ones, which the authors read as glycine's absent side chain letting it pack tightly while its backbone hydrogen-bonds across the interface; preferred aromatic–aromatic and hydrophobic–hydrophobic couplings, though not involving Ile or Leu; and, strikingly, **charged–charged couples underrepresented**, with polar–polar underrepresented less strongly. The complementary constraint is satisfaction of buried polar atoms: [[boyken-2016-hydrogen-bond-network-oligomers]] establishes that buried polar interactions are hard to design because a hydrogen bond is partially covalent and so restricts donors and acceptors to narrow ranges of orientation and distance, and because nearly all buried polar atoms must find a partner or incur a considerable desolvation penalty — and that a hydrogen-bond network is inherently *many-body*, which is why conventional design algorithms expressing energy as a sum of pairwise residue interactions handle it badly.

Taken together, these two sources give the chapter the distinction it needs between **affinity** and **specificity**. Packing density and buried area buy affinity; the identity and satisfaction of polar contacts buy discrimination between similar targets. [[boyken-2016-hydrogen-bond-network-oligomers]] demonstrated the point in the oligomer setting, where designs with hydrophobic cores each folded correctly yet cross-assembled promiscuously until hydrogen-bond networks were installed, after which the networks conferred interaction specificity in vivo. The binder literature's specificity evidence is thinner and is almost always a negative control rather than a designed property: no binding of a PTH binder to PTHrP at 34% identity, and no cross-reactivity of an NPY binder to PYY ([[vasquez-torres-2023-helical-peptide-binders]]); no binding of a PD-1 binder to porcine PD-1 ([[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]]). §13 returns to whether specificity is ever designed or only observed.

What no ingested source supplies is the kinetic half. Every affinity in this chapter is an equilibrium constant; association and dissociation rates appear nowhere, and a source on binding kinetics is flagged in §14.

## 3. The pre-generative era — grafting a binding mode

The founding strategy was to transplant a binding mode that already existed. The designer's problem was then not *what interaction to make* but *what protein to put it on*: given a functional site — a binding motif, a catalytic constellation — build a scaffold that presents it in the right geometry.

[[wang-2022-functional-site-scaffolding]] is the deep-learning generalisation of that idea and the wiki's only ingested source for the era, which is a real limitation discussed in §14. The concept page is [[motif-scaffolding]]. The structural point for this chapter is that grafting bounds the accessible space by the space of *known* interactions: you can only scaffold a motif you already possess, so targets with no characterised binding partner are out of reach, and the designed interface is the natural one plus whatever the scaffold adds around it.

Two traces of the era survive in the sources as quantitative baselines. [[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl]] describes inheriting a Rosetta motif-grafting pipeline whose success rates were low enough that campaigns were screened rather than shortlisted, and [[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]] later measured its own 19% hit rate as **roughly two orders of magnitude above the previous Rosetta-based method on the same five targets** — which places the grafting-era rate at a few tenths of a percent on those targets, by that assay. The era's throughput signature is equally stark: [[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]] reports testing fewer than 100 designs per target "compared to thousands in previous studies".

Grafting did not disappear. It is the backbone of §6: [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]] matches surface descriptors to find binding *seeds* and then grafts them onto scaffolds with Rosetta MotifGraft against a database of 1,300 monomeric scaffolds plus designed-miniprotein and AlphaFold-proteome databases. And it remains the highest-affinity route in the diffusion literature: the tightest binders in [[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]] are not free-generated at all but **scaffolded around the p53 helix** that binds MDM2, reaching 0.5 and 0.7 nM against a native affinity of 600 nM. The wiki's reading is that this is a standing result the field has not fully absorbed — when a natural binding mode exists, building around it still beats inventing one.

## 4. Deep learning enters as a filter, not a generator

The pivotal move in this chapter is not a generative model. It is the insertion of a structure predictor **in front of the ordering decision**, and it is the best-sourced section here.

[[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl]] keeps the Rosetta binder pipeline and makes two substitutions. [[proteinmpnn]] replaces Rosetta for [[sequence-design]], greatly cutting compute; and [[jumper-2021-alphafold2]] (with RoseTTAFold as a cross-check) is used to score two distinct probabilities before anything is synthesised — that the designed sequence **folds** to the intended monomer, and that it **binds** the target in the designed pose. The reported effect of the filter is a **~10× increase in experimental success rate** ([[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl]]).

The supplement is where the discipline becomes operational, and three details from it are load-bearing for everything downstream ([[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl-supp]]):

- **The two metrics.** Monomer **pLDDT** answers "does it fold as designed?"; **pAE_interaction** answers "does it dock as designed?". Figure S6's ROC and true-positive-rate analysis quantifies the discriminative power of each, which is the empirical justification for using them as go/no-go gates rather than as soft rankings ([[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl-supp]]).
- **The operational success criterion** is a **yeast-surface-display SC50 < 4 µM**. The ~10× improvement is defined against that bar and against no other ([[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl]], [[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl-supp]]).
- **The practical trick that makes complex prediction reliable enough to filter on**: AlphaFold2 is run on the complex **with the target as a template and the Rosetta design model as an initial guess**. This is absent from the main text and is the difference between a usable and an unusable interface score ([[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl-supp]]).

Figure S7 adds a prospective comparison across three targets showing [[proteinmpnn]] is not merely cheaper than Rosetta FastDesign but at least comparable in experimental success once deep-learning filtering is applied ([[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl-supp]]).

Two consequences deserve stating. First, the quantity that improved tenfold was the **yield of the shortlist**, not the quality of the generator — the generator was unchanged. Second, this is the moment the field acquired an oracle, and §10 is about what happens when a community optimises against one.

## 5. Hotspot-conditioned diffusion — the scaffold requirement dissolves

[[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]] removes the requirement that the binder resemble anything. [[rfdiffusion]] fine-tunes the RoseTTAFold structure-prediction network on a denoising task, so that noised residue frames are iteratively denoised into a coherent backbone, and conditioning inputs steer where it goes.

The conditioning machinery for binders is specific and is mapped in the supplement ([[watson-2023-rfdiffusion-supp]]): **interface hotspots** — the target residues the binder should contact — plus **secondary-structure and block-adjacency conditioning**, plus a dedicated **PPI fine-tune** of the base model. That last item is easy to miss and worth flagging: binder design was not a free capability of unconditional RFdiffusion but a separately trained mode, which bears on how far the paper's "single general framework" claim extends. A further fine-tuned model adds coarse-grained control over binder scaffold **topology**, so a fold can be requested alongside the hotspots ([[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]]).

The campaign, in its own units. Five targets chosen to match the prior Rosetta benchmark — influenza A H1 haemagglutinin, IL-7Rα, PD-L1, insulin receptor and TrkA — with designs generated both with and without fold conditioning, filtered by AlphaFold2 confidence in the interface and the monomer, and **95 designs selected per target** for experimental characterisation. Binding was assessed by single-point biolayer interferometry at 10 µM binder, with success defined as response at or above 50% of the positive control's maximum. The result: **19% overall**, called a conservative estimate because some designs that bound had too little material to screen at 10 µM; binders for **all five targets**; and full BLI titrations on a subset giving **nanomolar affinities with no experimental optimisation**, including haemagglutinin and IL-7Rα binders at roughly **30 nM** ([[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]]).

The motif-scaffolded arm of the same paper is the stronger result and is discussed in §3: scaffolding the p53 helix against MDM2 gave **55 of 95 designs binding**, 32 of them monomeric, with **0.5 and 0.7 nM** affinities against a 600 nM native interaction and on average 31% more interface surface area than the native helix ([[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]]).

The *in silico* criteria used to select those 95 designs per target are recorded as **pAE < 5, pLDDT > 90 and RMSD < ~1 Å** ([[watson-2023-rfdiffusion-supp]]). They matter because they are not the criteria anyone else uses: [[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]] requires pLDDT > 90, interface pAE < 7.0 and binder RMSD < 1.5 Å, and [[cho-2025-protein-hunter]] requires interface PAE ≤ 1.5 and ipTM ≥ 0.8 for small-molecule work. The thresholds are close enough to look comparable and different enough that they are not.

**Hotspot compliance separates generation from hallucination.** [[lin-2026-genie3]] reports that for the H1 and IL-17A targets "a sizable proportion of otherwise successful BindCraft designs fail to bind the specified interface", and offers an architectural rather than incidental explanation: in hallucination, hotspot compliance is "an additional ad hoc loss term that is optimized during hallucination", whereas in a conditional generative model hotspots "are provided as inputs to the model during training and generation, resulting in a shift in the underlying distribution of the generative process towards compliant designs." The same paper shows the specification can be improved post hoc by two heuristics that expand it: **extended hotspots**, adding any target surface residue within 6 Å (Cβ–Cβ) of a user hotspot, where surface means relative solvent accessibility ≥ 0.25 and SASA ≥ 10 Å²; and **convergent hotspots**, adding interface residues found in the intersection of all successful designs from previous rounds. Both raise success rates, convergent substantially more so on multimeric targets, and applied iteratively to TNFα — the hardest target in the set — the success rate climbs nearly monotonically with design round ([[lin-2026-genie3]]).

**How hotspots are actually enforced varies more than the papers suggest.** [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] audits four generative pipelines and finds four mechanisms: Protpardelle-1c pins the target as a *motif to scaffold* via an atomistic contig and carries a placement diagnostic (`ca_motif_sample_rmsd`, ~0.030 Å on working runs); Proteína-Complexa supplies the target as *context* with `binder_gen_only: True` and — consequentially — **re-generates rather than copies** the target chains; RFdiffusion3 specifies hotspots at the level of **individual sidechain atoms**, mapping each residue to named atoms; and ProteinHunter supplies **no target structure at all**. In all four, hotspot conditioning is **soft guidance rather than a hard constraint** ([[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]]). That report also records that Protpardelle's global secondary-structure proportion control (`binder_sse_prop`, quantised to 0.1) is the only confirmed fold lever among them, with no counterpart in the other three — a practical narrowing of the topology conditioning that [[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]] introduced.

## 6. Surface fingerprints — the independent lineage

Everything in §4, §5 and §7 descends from one laboratory's stack. This section is the chapter's control on whether that stack's success reflects its method or its choice of targets.

[[gainza-2020-masif]] proposes that a protein's **molecular surface** is fingerprinted with patterns of chemical and geometric features that reveal what it will interact with, and that proteins **with no sequence homology** undergoing similar interactions display similar patterns. The surface is decomposed into overlapping radial patches of fixed **geodesic** radius (9 Å or 12 Å, application-dependent), each vertex is assigned precomputed geometric and chemical features — hydropathy index, continuum electrostatics among them — and geodesic convolutional layers learn to embed each patch into a vector descriptor, trained end-to-end per task so descriptors are task-specific rather than universal. The geodesic parameterisation is the load-bearing choice and the supplement shows why with a single figure: on trypsin, a 12 Å *Euclidean* patch centred in the interface pocket reaches points on an entirely different face of the protein, while the 12 Å *geodesic* patch stays on the interacting face ([[gainza-2020-masif-supp]]).

Three applications were demonstrated — pocket–ligand similarity, protein-binding **site** prediction, and ultrafast surface **search** for complex configurations ([[gainza-2020-masif]]) — with MaSIF-site reaching ROC AUC 0.86 with three convolutional layers against 0.77 with one, over 2,192,870 test-set surface points ([[gainza-2020-masif-supp]]).

[[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]] turns this into **MaSIF-seed**: fingerprint a chosen target patch, then evaluate fingerprint complementarity against a large library of candidate binding motifs — both α-helical and β-strand — harvested from the PDB, and graft the hits onto scaffolds. Patches are 12 Å radius capturing on average nearly 400 Å² of surface, chosen for consistency with the buried surface areas of real interfaces.

The conceptual inversion is worth stating precisely, because it is the section's reason for existing. Hotspot-conditioned diffusion tells a generator *where* to bind and asks it to invent a binder. Surface-fingerprint design **searches for what is complementary** to a given patch and then builds a scaffold around the hit. The epitope is an input in both cases; what differs is whether the binding chemistry is generated or retrieved.

**Benchmark.** Recovering the native binder in the correct conformation (interface RMSD < 3 Å) from co-crystal structures among 1,000 decoys, MaSIF-seed placed the correct answer top-1 in **18 of 31** helical cases and **41 of 83** non-helical cases, against **3/31 and 7/83** for ZDock and **6/31 and 21/83** for ZDock+ZRank2 — at average times of 15 and 118 minutes against 2,206–2,946 minutes ([[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]]). The speed comparison needs a caveat the main text omits: the supplement records that ZDock was run on a 96-vCPU cloud server with times measured as CPU user time ([[gainza-2020-masif-supp]]), so this is partly a GPU-versus-CPU comparison.

**Experimental results, on four therapeutically serious targets.** For the SARS-CoV-2 receptor-binding domain, MaSIF-site predicted a site distinct from the ACE2 footprint but overlapping enough to inhibit, and MaSIF-seed returned **7,713 binding seeds** for it; the initial design bound at **4.6 µM** and an optimised version at **80 nM**, with a **cryo-EM structure at 2.9 Å local resolution** agreeing with the AlphaFold prediction at **1.4 Å interface RMSD**, one binder bound per RBD on the trimeric spike. That design engages the RBD through small residues with a buried interface area of **1,452 Å²** — smaller than the ~2,071 ± 456 Å² typical of antibodies — and as an Fc fusion neutralised most variants of concern except those carrying L452R, with potency comparable to the clinical antibody imdevimab; the binding mode was near-identical between wild-type and Omicron complexes at 1.4 Å backbone RMSD ([[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]]).

PD-L1 was attacked **because** its PD-1-engaged site is "extremely flat at the structural level" — the class of surface that resists small molecules. Designs progressed from **2 µM** to **256 nM**, **374 nM** and **120 nM** across library and site-saturation optimisation, with **crystal structures** matching the computational models at **0.8 Å RMSD** and 1.0–1.9 Å interface RMSD, and site-saturation mutagenesis confirming that the four hotspot residues MaSIF-seed had placed were **crucial** — any other residue at those positions abolished binding ([[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]]). That last result is the chapter's only direct experimental validation of placed hotspots, and it is the experimental counterpart of the packing-versus-ΔΔG argument in §2.

**One-shot design.** The paper names its own earlier limitation — designs still required in vitro evolution to reach characterisable affinity — and addresses it with a structurally diverse seed library and design biased for polar interface contacts. Screening the top **2,000** designed sequences per target by yeast display with deep sequencing against PD-L1, PD-1 and CTLA-4 gave binders for **all three**. The best PD-1 binder bound at **K_D 4.2 ± 2 µM with no experimental optimisation at all**, comparable to the native PD-L1–PD-1 interaction at 8.2 µM, improving to submicromolar with two mutations from the SSM data; a β-sheet-mediated PD-L1 binder reached an apparent **21.8 nM** on yeast while adopting a backbone conformation **different from the native** PD-1–PD-L1 interaction ([[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]]).

The homology-independence of the representation ([[gainza-2020-masif]]) and the strictness of its evaluation splits are what make this lineage a real control. The supplement reports that test sets were split by hierarchical clustering on TM-score matrices, and that a **stricter split removing every test structure with TM-score > 0.5 to any training member did not affect performance** — MaSIF-site from 359 proteins to 169, MaSIF-search from 957 to 635 ([[gainza-2020-masif-supp]]). For a wiki that repeatedly asks whether a design method has memorised the PDB, this is the strongest such control in any ingested source.

## 7. Beyond protein targets — all-atom and ligand conditioning

A protein target can be represented adequately by its backbone. A small molecule cannot. The all-atom transition therefore matters for binder design specifically, and not merely as an accuracy improvement.

[[krishna-2024-rosettafold-all-atom]] generalises the Baker-lab prediction and design stack to all-atom, ligand-aware territory, and its design arm **RFdiffusionAA** diffuses a binder backbone *around* a small molecule. Experimental validation by crystallography and binding measurements covers three ligands: the cardiac therapeutic **digoxigenin**, the cofactor **heme**, and the light-harvesting **bilin** ([[krishna-2024-rosettafold-all-atom]]). The supplement carries the featurisation — atomised groups, bonds, chirality, metals — and the per-ligand binder protocols ([[krishna-2024-rosettafold-all-atom-supp]]).

[[butcher-2025-rfdiffusion3]] unifies the modalities in one all-atom token-and-atom model: protein–protein, protein–DNA and protein–small-molecule interfaces from a single generator, matching or beating prior methods on **protein–protein interaction, DNA-binder, small-molecule and atomic-motif-enzyme benchmarks at roughly one-tenth the computational cost** ([[butcher-2025-rfdiffusion3]], [[butcher-2025-rfdiffusion3-supp]]). The cost reduction is best read not as a convenience but as an enabler of the search strategies in §11.

The all-atom turn is also where the generative and the predictive lineages converge: [[abramson-2024-alphafold3]] is the prediction-side counterpart, and [[chu-2024-protpardelle]] reaches all-atom generation by a different route, superposition-based rather than tokenised. [[ingraham-2023-chroma]] contributes the orthogonal idea that conditioning should be programmable rather than task-specific. The concept page is [[diffusion-generative-design]].

**A fourth route disputes the premise.** The three routes above all gave up SE(3)-equivariance to get atomistic detail, a move [[lin-2026-genie3]] describes as the field following "the evolution of AlphaFold2 to AlphaFold3" and eschewing "data-efficient SE(3)-equivariance". Genie 3 keeps the equivariance and gets all-atom detail from a **branched-polymer** treatment instead — sidechain frames built off the backbone frame in `atom14` ordering — with **partial atomization**, so only residues whose sidechains are actually known are represented atomistically. The equivariant frame representation it generalises dates to [[lin-2023-genie]], whose 4.1 M-parameter model reached 0.790 designability against RFdiffusion's 0.951 at 59.8 M — the parameter-efficiency observation that became this lineage's thesis. That it then outperforms the non-equivariant models on binder design (§11) is the chapter's reason for treating the architectural consensus as unsettled rather than superseded.

One operational caveat on representation, from outside the literature. [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] records RFdiffusion3's output as **observed all-atom at 6.4 atoms per residue with full sidechains and a low-entropy co-designed sequence** — while noting that RFD3's own quality-control comments describe the same output as "backbone-only diffusion output", a discrepancy that report leaves unresolved. It also notes that in practice the distinction is moot for that pipeline, because the next stage discards the generator's sequence and re-derives it from backbone alone with ProteinMPNN. §9 takes up why that step is not as innocuous as it sounds.

## 8. Hard target classes

### 8.1 Flexible and disordered targets

Many peptide hormones adopt an α-helix only on binding their receptor; free in solution they are floppy. [[vasquez-torres-2023-helical-peptide-binders]] states the difficulty as two compounding problems: the picomolar-affinity hyper-stable 50–65-residue minibinders that work against folded proteins have shapes suited to protein surfaces rather than to an extended helix, and peptides offer **fewer residues to interact with**.

The paper's three design generations are the cleanest affinity ladder in the chapter. **Parametric groove scaffolds** gave 8 of 12 designs binding for each of PTH, glucagon and NPY — but **micromolar only**. **RFjoint inpainting plus ProteinMPNN redesign**, extending the binder interface, gave 44 binders from 192 designs tested against PTH with the best at **6.1 nM**, about three orders of magnitude better than the parametric starting point; the control that matters is that **ProteinMPNN redesign of the original interface alone did not measurably increase affinity**, so the gain came from extending the interface rather than from better sequence design. The same route gave 231 nM for glucagon and 3.5 µM for NPY from 96 designs. **De novo RFdiffusion from noise** then gave **2.7 nM for secretin**, sub-100 nM for GLP1 and GIP, and **7 nM for the Bid BH3 peptide** — unstructured in isolation — a *higher* affinity than Bid's native partner Mcl-1 ([[vasquez-torres-2023-helical-peptide-binders]]).

**Partial diffusion** is the operator that closed the gap: noise an existing design partway, then re-denoise, diversifying the fold by an amount set by the noise level and "regularising" backbones so that ProteinMPNN sequence design on them is more designable. Applied to the 231 nM glucagon and 3.5 µM NPY binders it gave 25 of 96 designs binding glucagon and 20 of 96 binding NPY at 10 nM peptide, and final affinities of **5.6 nM for NPY** and, for glucagon, **below the limit of detection in the picomolar range**; a Bim binder reached **K_D < 500 pM**, described as the highest-affinity designed peptide binders then reported. Crystal structures at **1.95 Å and 1.81 Å** matched the design models at 0.72 Å and 0.6 Å RMSD ([[vasquez-torres-2023-helical-peptide-binders]]).

The applications close the loop to diagnostics: the 6.1 nM PTH binder was grafted into a luminescent caged reporter giving a **10 nM limit of detection with ~21-fold activation**, and the picomolar PTH and glucagon binders were used for immunoaffinity enrichment of peptide hormones **from human serum** followed by mass-spectrometric detection ([[vasquez-torres-2023-helical-peptide-binders]]).

[[stark-2025-boltzgen]] pushes the same frontier past peptides to **intrinsically disordered regions**. Twenty thousand peptide designs of 40–80 residues were generated against the disordered region of NPM1, specified to engage the disordered region while avoiding its structured β-sheet — which tasks the model with predicting how the disordered region will *become* structured in the presence of the designed binder. Five designs were tested, with reported evidence of binding **in live cells**; the paper is explicit that the in-cell experiment does not confirm that binding occurs specifically at the disordered region ([[stark-2025-boltzgen]]).

### 8.2 Antibodies and CDR loops

Antibody design is the modality most therapeutic interest attaches to, and it is a genuinely different problem: the designable region is a set of **loops on a fixed framework** rather than a free backbone, and the interactions are loop-mediated rather than the secondary-structure-paired interfaces miniprotein design exploits.

**What was missing, and why.** [[bennett-2026-de-novo-antibodies]] opens on the observation that methods for the *first* stage of antibody development — discovering antibodies that bind a **specified epitope** — "do not exist", so discovery proceeds by immunization or library screening "**followed by subsequent epitope mapping**". The epitope is normally found after the fact. Prior deep-learning work either sampled alternative *native* CDR loops to improve affinity, or used vanilla RFdiffusion, which was not built for loop-mediated interfaces.

**How it is solved.** A **fine-tuned RFdiffusion2** trained predominantly on antibody complexes, in which the **framework sequence and structure are supplied as conditioning** while the antibody structure is corrupted — and supplied **global-frame-invariantly**, through the template track as a 2D matrix, so framework and target templates carry no relative position ([[bennett-2026-de-novo-antibodies]]). That is the architectural trick: the framework's internal structure stays fixed, inheriting a therapeutic scaffold, while the **rigid-body placement of the antibody against the epitope is designed rather than assumed**. CDR sequences come from [[dauparas-2022-proteinmpnn]]. The filter is a **separately fine-tuned RoseTTAFold2** that must model the CDRs and find the pose given the target and epitope, and which outperformed the then-available models "especially at CDR H3" — the longest, most variable loop and the usual failure point. Its calibration is conditional rather than absolute: at **mean pAE < 10, 80.3% of structures are within 2 Å when 100% of hotspots are satisfied** ([[bennett-2026-de-novo-antibodies-supp]]).

A detail from the supplement changes how the generalisation should be read: training used **SAbDab, a TCR/MHC-peptide set, and loop-mediated interactions in the PDB** ([[bennett-2026-de-novo-antibodies-supp]]). The prior is over loop-mediated recognition broadly, not over antibodies — which is why the same network could be pointed at a peptide–MHC complex.

**Results, in the chapter's units.** Six targets (TcdB, influenza H1 haemagglutinin, RSV sites I and III, SARS-CoV-2 RBD, IL-7Rα), screened either at **9,000 designs per target by yeast display** or **95 per target** by *E. coli* expression and single-concentration SPR. **Success rates per target range from 0% to 2%** ([[bennett-2026-de-novo-antibodies]]). Initial designs land at tens to hundreds of nanomolar: **78 nM** for haemagglutinin, **260 nM** for TcdB, **5.5 µM** for RBD. **OrthoRep** continuous hypermutation then buys roughly **two orders of magnitude**, giving single-digit nanomolar binders that retain epitope selectivity. scFvs assembled from designed heavy- and light-chain CDRs in **~10-million-member combinatorial libraries** reached **72 nM** (68 nM as full IgG1) against TcdB.

**The specificity controls are the strongest in the chapter.** The TcdB epitope was the **Frizzled interface, for which no antibody or VHH exists in the PDB**. Binding was abolished by competition with a structurally characterised de novo binder to the same site (FZD48, PDB 9CM5; AHB2, PDB 7UHB for RBD); there was **no binding to the 70%-homologous toxin TcsL**; and the VHH **neutralised TcdB toxicity in CSPG4-knockout cells at EC50 460 nM** ([[bennett-2026-de-novo-antibodies]]). §9's open question about whether specificity is designed or observed is answered here more directly than anywhere else in the chapter.

**Atomic accuracy, by cryo-EM.** VHH_flu_01 on natively glycosylated trimeric haemagglutinin at **3.0 Å**: backbone RMSD **1.45 Å** to the design, **CDR3 RMSD 0.8 Å**, with named designed residues seen contacting the stem epitope as intended. Roughly **66% of particles** carried up to two VHHs per trimer, the partial occupancy attributed to the **N296 glycan** occluding the epitope in unbound subunits and reorienting on binding. For one TcdB scFv, high-resolution data verified the designed conformations of **all six CDR loops** ([[bennett-2026-de-novo-antibodies]]).

**What the number means.** 0–2% against the **19%** hotspot-conditioned miniprotein rate from the same lab ([[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]]) is the clearest measure in the chapter of what the antibody format costs — roughly an order of magnitude in yield per design. Set against [[stark-2025-boltzgen]], which obtained nanomolar nanobodies for 66% of targets from 15 tested but **60,000 generated**, the two are closer than they look: both reach useful binders by screening deeply rather than by high per-design success. The wiki's reading is that de novo design has displaced stage 1 of antibody discovery — epitope-specific generation — and has not displaced stage 2, since maturation still supplies the final two orders of magnitude.

Adjacent capabilities elsewhere: [[cho-2025-protein-hunter]] demonstrates antibody CDR redesign, and [[stark-2025-boltzgen]] treats nanobodies as a first-class modality by fixing a framework and replacing its three CDRs with random-length loops from four default scaffolds.

### 8.3 Modality as an input

[[stark-2025-boltzgen]] is the first ingested source for which the binder's modality is specified rather than assumed. Design is controlled by a **specification language** over covalent bonds, structure constraints and binding sites, so a cyclic peptide is requested by declaring a covalent bond and a nanobody by fixing a framework. Design and structure prediction are unified in one model, which also reaches state-of-the-art folding performance ([[stark-2025-boltzgen]]).

Its **novelty control** is the methodological reason this chapter leans on it. Nine targets were chosen for which **no protein with >30% sequence identity exists in a bound context anywhere in the PDB**. Against each, 60,000 nanobodies were generated *without specifying any binding site* and **15 tested**; separately 60,000 proteins of length 80–140, again 15 tested. The outcome was **nanomolar binders for 6 of 9 targets (66%) for nanobodies and the same 66% for protein binders** by SPR and BLI ([[stark-2025-boltzgen]]). On five benchmark targets with known high-affinity binders, testing at most 20 proteins and 15 nanobodies gave nanomolar binders against **80%** of targets for both modalities.

The modality sweep and its affinity gradient are informative in themselves ([[stark-2025-boltzgen]]): nanomolar against protein targets; **0.41 and 4.4 µM** against melittin and nanomolar or micromolar against three bioactive peptides from six designs each; **7 binders from 29** linear peptides against RagC with the best at 3.5 µM; disulfide-cyclised peptides against the RagA:RagC dimer giving binders but only **80 µM and 164 µM** as the two tightest resolved; **50–150 µM** against rucaparib and **30–250 µM** against a rhodamine derivative; and **19.5%** of antimicrobial peptide designs inhibiting cell growth more than fourfold against GyrA. The paper is candid that prior specialist work reached low-nanomolar on rucaparib through an expert-guided approach, and claims only that a general-purpose model achieves moderate affinity with simpler customisation. The wiki's reading of the gradient is that affinity still tracks how much surface the modality has to work with — which is the §2 packing argument reappearing as a constraint on modality rather than on epitope.

One internal inconsistency should be recorded rather than smoothed: the paper's summary states binders for **14 of 28** tested disulfide-bonded designs, while its §2.6 states 14 binders from **24** tested ([[stark-2025-boltzgen]]).

## 9. Sequence design at an interface is not the monomer problem

An interface residue is solvent-exposed in the monomer and buried in the complex. A model conditioned on the monomer alone is therefore being asked the wrong question, and this section collects the evidence that the sequence step is a distinct problem rather than a detail.

[[dauparas-2022-proteinmpnn]] is the workhorse, and its relevance here is that it was built to be conditioned: sequence design on a fixed backbone with the option to tie or fix positions. [[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl-supp]] establishes prospectively that it is at least comparable to Rosetta FastDesign for binders once filtering is applied, across three targets, while being far cheaper.

The **interface prior** is the subtlest issue in the chapter, and the evidence for it arrives from the opposite direction. [[anishchenko-2021-hallucination]] designed monomers by hallucination and found that many came out as **oligomers in solution** — one design crystallising as a dimer — and diagnosed the cause precisely: trRosetta had been trained on native structures including many homo- and hetero-oligomers, so it never learned to disfavour surface hydrophobics to the degree required for soluble monomers, having absorbed **the sequence signatures of native protein–protein interfaces** from the PDB. The rescue was manual substitution of exposed hydrophobics with polar residues, shown by paired before/after SEC traces ([[anishchenko-2021-hallucination-supp]]).

The wiki's reading is that this is a *resource* for binder design and a liability for monomer design, and that no ingested source settles which way it cuts when a generator fine-tuned from a structure predictor is asked for an interface. §13 records it as open.

Two operational findings sharpen the section beyond what the published sources establish. First, the step that discards a generator's own sequence is not free: [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] records that in the RFdiffusion3 pipeline the generator's co-designed sequence is **discarded** at `mpnn_redesign`, because ProteinMPNN is backbone-only by construction — so whatever sidechain information the all-atom generator produced is not used. Second, and more consequentially, that report finds that Proteína-Complexa's **co-designed sequence carries an antigen-copying defect**: 48 contiguous target residues reproduced inside a 79-residue binder, correlating with the search reward at ρ +0.54, severe enough that the project's headline metric is computed on MPNN redesigns because the co-designed sequence cannot be substituted ([[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]]).

That finding stands in **direct contradiction** to the published claim about the same model. [[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]] reports that Complexa's self-generated sequences outperform all baselines including ProteinMPNN redesign, concluding that "such additional steps [are] unnecessary". The wiki's reading is that these are not two measurements of one quantity but measurements of two different things — an *in silico* benchmark success rate on standard targets, and an operated pipeline's behaviour on a hard real target — and that the gap between them is the single most instructive fact available to this chapter. It is taken up again in §12.

## 10. The oracle, its metrics, and its failure modes

The field runs on two numbers: a monomer confidence and an interface error estimate. §4 established their provenance ([[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl-supp]]). This section is about what they cannot see and what happens when they are optimised against.

**What the metrics measure.** pLDDT is a local-confidence estimate from [[jumper-2021-alphafold2]]; the interface predicted-aligned-error family answers whether the predictor places the two chains in the designed relative pose. Neither is an affinity. [[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]] states the empirical basis for using them at all — that interface confidence and alignment scores from structure predictors correlate with wet-lab success when evaluated on designed binder–target pairs — and treats them as standard *in silico* metrics on that basis.

**Adversarial overfitting is the known failure, and it is severe.** [[wicky-2022-symmetric-assembly-design]] ran hundreds of Monte Carlo steps against AlphaFold2 with a symmetry objective, selected 150 designs with pLDDT > 0.7 and pTM > 0.7, and found that **virtually none expressed solubly** — median soluble yield 9 mg/L, with none of the marginally soluble ones showing both the expected oligomerisation state and a consistent circular-dichroism profile. The diagnosis was overfitting to the oracle: many optimisation steps against a confidence metric generate sequences the network confidently predicts as structured but which have aberrant biophysical properties. The fix was to discard the hallucinated sequences, keep the backbones, and redesign with [[dauparas-2022-proteinmpnn]] using **dozens rather than hundreds of evaluations** — after which 71 of 96 expressed at high levels with a median yield of 247 mg/L, a ~27-fold increase ([[wicky-2022-symmetric-assembly-design]]).

The structural lesson generalises past symmetry: **an oracle used as a gate is reliable in a way that an oracle used as an objective is not**, and the number of evaluations is the control variable. [[frank-2024-relaxed-sequence-optimization]] pushes the objective route further with gradients in a relaxed sequence space rather than MCMC, and its supplement records an interface filtering cascade ([[frank-2024-relaxed-sequence-optimization-supp]]).

**Three independent demonstrations that the filter, not the generator, sets the hit rate.**

- [[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl]]: inserting the filter raised experimental success ~10× with the generator unchanged.
- [[stark-2025-boltzgen]]: an earlier version of the model "suffered from a serious flaw resulting in close-to-random ranking and filtering", and for designs from that version **no hits were found** — the generator being substantially the same. This is the cleanest natural experiment in the chapter.
- [[stark-2025-boltzgen]] again, by ratio: **60,000 designs generated per target, 15 tested**. Every published binder success rate is a rate measured after a filter of roughly that severity — 4,000:1 — and this is the only source that states the magnitude.

**The oracle's precision has now been measured, and it is low.** [[lin-2026-genie3]] reports that "all evaluation pipelines exhibit low precision (maximum of 12%) and completely fail to identify binders against H3, TGFβ, and TIE2", and states the consequence directly: this "place[s] a low ceiling on the efficiency of binder design using *in silico* oracles as filters." That is a harder number than anything in [[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl-supp]]'s ROC analysis, and it comes from a pipeline whose filtering power had been retrospectively calibrated against an experimentally validated dataset of both positives and negatives.

**Standard oracles are also blind to *where* a binder binds.** [[lin-2026-genie3]] found designs that passed an AlphaFold-Multimer benchmark while binding an **off-target site**, and repaired it by adding an on-target constraint — **≥80% of user-specified interface residues within 5 Å of the binder** by closest heavy atoms, or 100% for interfaces of three residues or fewer — calling the result the AF2M+ Benchmark and using it for all downstream analysis. This is the chapter's only ingested instance of a published filter being fixed *because* it was caught passing off-target binders, and it is the literature counterpart of the gate pathologies measured operationally in [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]].

**What the oracle still cannot see.** No ingested source uses a predictor to assess binding kinetics, aggregation propensity, expression yield, or off-target cross-reactivity. [[wicky-2022-symmetric-assembly-design]] is the proof that expression and solubility are invisible to it. And [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]] supplies the chapter's sharpest counterexample to oracle authority: for several of its experimentally confirmed binders the **AlphaFold predictions did not agree with the design models**, while competition and mutagenesis experiments nevertheless established that the designed interfaces were the ones in use — and the paper notes that AlphaFold agreement is "a predictive exercise" that "cannot be interpreted as absolute evidence that the designed binding mode is occurring, which will ultimately require an experimental structure."

**Circularity is a newer failure, and it arrives with unified models.** When the generator and the scorer share weights or training data, the score reports the generator's own objective back to it. [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] handles this explicitly for the BoltzGen arm, declaring its Boltz-2 scoring tier **circular** because Boltz-2 is a component of BoltzGen's own pipeline — with the asymmetric reading that "a BG advantage here is not evidence; a BG deficit would be" — and promoting AlphaFold2, which shares no weights or training pipeline, to primary binding tier for that arm. The same report records that several axes for that arm are **inflated** because the delivery's rank ordering is BoltzGen's own weighted rank, and that self-consistency RMSD is outright censored for it. Since [[stark-2025-boltzgen]] and [[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]] both make unification of design and prediction a selling point, the wiki's reading is that circularity is a structural consequence of the field's current direction rather than an implementation slip.

**And the gate can select against the thing it is meant to select for.** [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] reports that for the Complexa pipeline, placement and pass/fail are measured on *different structures* and agree at chance — Cohen's κ of +0.006 and −0.091 — because placement is read off the search pose while passing is read off AlphaFold2's independent re-dock of the redesigned sequence, and that **the AlphaFold2 gate depletes epitope contact rather than enriching it**. For ProteinHunter the same report records that moving a promotion gate from 0.8 to 0.7 shifted promotion from 14.1% to 43.8% on byte-matched generation, meaning the stricter gate had been **discarding on-epitope designs**.

## 11. Compute, search, and a false dichotomy

By 2025 the methodological split in binder design was between **generative** models trained on binder–target complexes and **hallucination** methods that optimise a sequence against a structure predictor's confidence. [[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]] argues the split is false, and the argument is this chapter's most useful reframing.

Its analogy is to language and image modelling, where a pretrained base model is combined with adaptive inference-time compute in one framework; by comparison, generative binder design is **pure training-time optimisation** and hallucination is **pure inference-time optimisation with no generative prior** ([[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]]). Proteína-Complexa supplies the missing combination in three parts: **Teddymer**, a large synthetic dataset built by treating the domain–domain interactions of computationally predicted monomers as surrogate binder–target complexes, used for pretraining before post-training on experimental multimers; a **partially latent** flow-matching architecture over a Cα coordinate variable plus per-residue latents decoded to all-atom structure and sequence; and inference-time optimisation adapted from the diffusion literature — **best-of-N, beam search, Feynman–Kac steering** (importance sampling of a tilted distribution) and **MCTS** — with interface pAE as the reward.

Without any test-time optimisation it reports 9.10 unique successes on self-generated sequences and 14.4 with full ProteinMPNN redesign, against 4.68 for RFdiffusion and 0.73 for Protpardelle-1c under MPNN redesign; with test-time search it reports beating the hallucination baselines **BindCraft** and **BoltzDesign** under **normalised compute budgets measured in test-time GPU-hours**, with simple best-of-N already sufficient on easy targets and the more elaborate searches paying off as targets harden ([[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]]). It claims to be the first systematic exploration of test-time scaling for protein design.

[[cho-2025-protein-hunter]] reaches the same unification from the opposite side and is the more surprising result. It is **fine-tuning-free**: start from an **all-X sequence**, let a diffusion-based structure predictor hallucinate a structure for it, then cycle sequence redesign with ProteinMPNN against structure re-prediction. There is no diffusion over coordinates and no training — the generative capacity is borrowed wholesale from a predictor's willingness to hallucinate. The X token is chosen empirically (X-initiated designs gave the highest ipTM and lowest loop fractions) and mechanistically (AF3-style models encode unknown PDB residues as X). Boltz-2 outperformed Chai-1 and AlphaFold3 as the hallucination substrate, which the paper attributes to Boltz-2 encoding each non-canonical residue as a single learned token, so X behaves as one residue, whereas atom-level tokenisation increases sampling complexity — and to AlphaFold3 having been fine-tuned on distilled AF2 predictions, which encourages spaghetti-like low-confidence regions and so suppresses the very hallucination the method depends on ([[cho-2025-protein-hunter]]).

Its results: higher ipTM than RFdiffusion on **9 of 11** protein targets with Boltz-2 and 7 of 11 with Chai-1; higher small-molecule success rates than RFdiffusionAA, BoltzDesign2 and its own single-pass mode on all four ligands tested, under criteria of backbone RMSD ≤ 1.5 Å, ligand RMSD ≤ 5 Å, interface PAE ≤ 1.5 and ipTM ≥ 0.8; plus cyclic peptides, multi-motif scaffolding, antibody CDR redesign and DNA/RNA binders. Its efficiency claim is that iterative cycling **reduces the need for extensive sequence sampling**, co-optimising structure and sequence across cycles. Its stated limitation is that it inherits the predictor's prior including its biases — structures from AF3-style models showed **lower diversity than RFdiffusion's**, with a preference for α-helical topologies ([[cho-2025-protein-hunter]]).

**A third answer: don't combine them, just win.** [[lin-2026-genie3]] declines the synthesis. Genie 3 is a pure diffusion model with no inference-time oracle optimisation in the loop, and on the ten AlphaProteo binder problems at a fixed budget of 200 generated structures per model per problem it **produces the most successful designs on 7 of 10, with BindCraft best on the remaining 3** — while RFdiffusion, BoltzGen and Proteína-Complexa are "substantially" behind both. Once success is normalised by per-sample runtime the gap over BindCraft widens, which is the paper's central claim: a generative model "can outperform a far more expensive hallucination-based model" ([[lin-2026-genie3]]). Against Proteína-Complexa specifically, on inference-time scaling across the same ten problems, Complexa wins one, ties two and loses seven — and **in four cases exhibits no scaling behaviour at all** while Genie 3 improves robustly with compute, with the gains concentrated in multimeric targets ([[lin-2026-genie3]]).

It also reverses the field's other recent architectural move. Where all-atom design has meant abandoning SE(3)-equivariance — "mirroring the evolution of AlphaFold2 to AlphaFold3" — Genie 3 keeps equivariance and reaches atomistic detail by treating the protein as a **branched polymer**: Frenet–Serret frames anchored at Cα for the backbone, then sidechain frames constructed from the backbone frame in `atom14` ordering, with **partial atomization** giving all-atom representation for known motif residues and backbone-only for scaffold residues ([[lin-2026-genie3]]). Its efficiency gain is separable from its architecture and worth noting on its own: moderating the reverse process by a **direction scale on the mean** rather than a noise scale on the variance allows Δt = 10, cutting sampling from 1,000 steps to 100 — **10× faster with negligible quality loss**.

**The wiki's reading.** The hallucination lineage in this wiki runs [[anishchenko-2021-hallucination]] → [[wicky-2022-symmetric-assembly-design]] → [[frank-2024-relaxed-sequence-optimization]], and its recurring failure was adversarial overfitting to the oracle (§10). [[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]] and [[cho-2025-protein-hunter]] suggest the problem was never the optimisation but the **absence of a prior to optimise inside**: search constrained to a learned manifold cannot easily reach the adversarial region. The first builds such a prior; the second borrows one from a predictor. [[lin-2026-genie3]] complicates that story rather than confirming it — it shows that a sufficiently good prior with *no* search at all beats the hallucination state of the art, which suggests the prior was doing most of the work in the combined methods too. Taken together, the wiki's reading is that the field's 2024–25 consensus (drop equivariance, adopt hallucination) was adopted faster than it was tested. Whether that is the right account of the overfitting failure is recorded as open in §13.

**What search costs in practice** is measurable only from the first-party source, and the numbers are sobering. Over the whole history of one project, [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] measures **21,707 Slurm tasks and 10,376.1 GPU-hours** (7,418.0 generation, 2,956.8 scoring) plus 127,987.1 CPU-hours — of which **303.7 GPU-hours were charged to failed tasks**. Per design: Protpardelle 8.40 GPU-minutes, ProteinHunter 15.40, RFdiffusion3 20.00, Complexa **54.60**. The search-heavy pipeline is roughly 6.5× the cost per candidate of the cheapest, before any consideration of how many candidates each needs.

That report also supplies the counter-example to naive scaling. Protpardelle, which scales by embarrassingly parallel replication up to 2,048 independent draws rather than guided search, was found on pool clustering to gain nothing from breadth: **tripling n on one recipe added zero new folds** ([[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]]). Independent draws and guided search are not interchangeable uses of the same compute, which is precisely [[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]]'s thesis arriving as an operational result.

## 12. What actually gets validated — and why the rates do not compare

### 12.1 The ladder

Across the sources, validation proceeds in a consistent order, and each rung proves something different.

**Display for hit rate.** Yeast surface display, with the operational bar **SC50 < 4 µM** in [[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl-supp]], and deep-sequencing readout over the top 2,000 designs per target in [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]]. **Single-point binding for triage.** Biolayer interferometry at 10 µM with success at ≥50% of the positive control's maximal response in [[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]]. **Titration for affinity.** SPR and BLI in [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]] and [[stark-2025-boltzgen]]; fluorescence polarisation in [[vasquez-torres-2023-helical-peptide-binders]]. **Behaviour.** Size-exclusion chromatography, monodispersity, thermostability and expression yield — the properties [[wicky-2022-symmetric-assembly-design]] showed the oracle cannot see. **Structure.** Crystallography at 1.81–1.95 Å in [[vasquez-torres-2023-helical-peptide-binders]] and 0.8 Å model agreement in [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]]; cryo-EM at 2.9 Å local resolution in [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]] and of a designed binder in complex with influenza haemagglutinin in [[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]]. **Specificity, as controls.** Knockout mutants, competition with clinical antibodies, and homologue non-binding in [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]]; related-peptide non-binding in [[vasquez-torres-2023-helical-peptide-binders]]. **Function.** Viral neutralisation in [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]]; biosensors and serum immunoaffinity enrichment in [[vasquez-torres-2023-helical-peptide-binders]]; in-cell binding and growth inhibition in [[stark-2025-boltzgen]].

### 12.2 Why the rates do not compare

Here are the chapter's headline success figures, each in its own units. They are deliberately **not** aggregated.

One partial counterexample now exists, and it deserves naming before the incomparabilities. [[lin-2026-genie3]] runs **five methods — Genie 3, BindCraft, RFdiffusion, BoltzGen and Proteína-Complexa — on one shared ten-problem set, at a fixed budget of 200 generated structures per model per problem, through one evaluation pipeline**. That is the closest thing to a sound cross-method comparison the wiki holds. Its limits are equally clear: it is an *in silico* comparison, its oracle tops out at 12% precision and fails entirely on three targets, and the single wet-lab datapoint it carries comes from a blind competition whose comparator hit rates "encompass diverse generation and filtering procedures performed by groups with varying levels of expertise" ([[lin-2026-genie3]]).

Four distinct incomparabilities are visible. The **assay** differs (display SC50, single-point BLI, titrated K_D, *in silico* confidence). The **bar** differs even within one assay family (19% at ≥50% of a positive control is not the same event as "nanomolar by SPR"). The **denominator** differs in kind: 95 post-filter designs in [[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]] versus 15 designs selected from 60,000 in [[stark-2025-boltzgen]] versus 2,000 in [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]]. And the **target sets** differ, which matters most of all: [[stark-2025-boltzgen]]'s 66% is against targets with no >30%-identity bound-context homologue in the PDB, while its own 80% is against benchmark targets with known high-affinity binders — the same model, the same assay, a 14-point spread attributable entirely to target difficulty.

### 12.3 The first-party ledger

[[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] is the only source in the wiki that attacks this problem directly rather than inheriting it, and it does so by refusing to rank: in place of a results table it carries a **comparability ledger** stating, per pipeline, what its rate is a fraction of, why it cannot sit beside another's, and what would have to be held fixed for a fair comparison. Its findings on six pipelines run against one target class:

- **Protpardelle-1c** — denominator is designs generated, with no pre-scoring gate, the cleanest in the set; but the metric is complex-level self-consistency from the model's own sequence, which asks whether a design is self-consistent, not whether it binds ([[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]]).
- **Proteína-Complexa** — denominator is finalists surviving a filter capped at `filter_samples_limit: 1000`, so campaigns at n=16 and n=512 have **incomparable denominators**; and the rate is computed on redesigned rather than co-designed sequences, measuring the redesign pathway rather than the generator ([[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]]).
- **ProteinHunter** — denominator is designs *promoted* by a generation-stage gate, with **79% of tasks lost before any validation runs** ([[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]]).
- **RFdiffusion3** — clean denominator at n=64, but a different target set entirely, and on three of five benchmark targets the 0/64 result is **unmeasurable rather than undesignable**, because ESMFold cannot rebuild those targets (median target prediction RMSD 2.18, 9.54 and 17.68 Å); those rows carry binder-only self-consistency at 84–94% instead ([[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]]).
- **DiffuseBio and BoltzGen** — pass rate is **banned by preregistration**, because both are post-filter deliveries with the generated pool withheld, so any rate would describe the supplier's selection rule rather than the method. Comparison is made instead as **paired deltas within each design across two target contexts**, which is immune to selection because each design is its own control ([[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]]).

That last device is the chapter's most transferable methodological point. When a denominator cannot be recovered, a **within-design paired contrast** can still be sound where a rate cannot.

### 12.4 Representation as a validity condition

The same report contributes an axis absent from the published literature: an audit of **what each pipeline stage can actually see** — all-atom, backbone, Cα-only or sequence-only — on the argument that a stage seeing only backbone cannot evaluate packing, a stage that rebuilds sidechains is inferring them from a sequence rather than observing them, and reading a packing or interface number off a backbone-only stage is "the single most common way to over-read a result" ([[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]]). Two concrete findings follow: in one pipeline the file from which every contact and interface-residue count is read is **Cα-only**, while the all-atom file goes to the sequence designer; in another the generator's sidechains are **discarded** before scoring. Given §2's result that interface energetics are a packing phenomenon correlating with occupied volume at r² = 0.94 ([[halperin-2004-interface-hot-spots]]), the wiki's reading is that atomic representation per stage is not a software detail but a **validity condition** on any interface number a pipeline reports.

## 13. Open questions

- **Why is the hit rate what it is?** No ingested source diagnoses *why* 19% of filtered RFdiffusion binders bind and 81% do not ([[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]]), or whether the failures are dominated by interface quality, expression, aggregation or wrong binding mode. The chapter's two mechanistic failure accounts — surface hydrophobicity inherited from training on native oligomers ([[anishchenko-2021-hallucination]]) and adversarial overfitting to the oracle ([[wicky-2022-symmetric-assembly-design]]) — are both about *hallucination*, and neither transfers cleanly to a conditional diffusion model that never optimises against a predictor.
- **Is specificity ever designed, or only observed?** Most specificity results in the chapter are negative controls run after the fact ([[vasquez-torres-2023-helical-peptide-binders]], [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]]). [[bennett-2026-de-novo-antibodies]] goes furthest the other way — an epitope with no antibody in the PDB, chosen in advance, confirmed by competition against a structurally characterised binder to that site, with non-binding to a 70%-homologous toxin and functional neutralisation in a receptor-knockout line — so epitope *targeting* is now demonstrably designed. Whether broader off-target cross-reactivity is designed remains untested. The one source that treats specificity as a design objective with its own machinery does so for oligomers, not binders ([[boyken-2016-hydrogen-bond-network-oligomers]]). No ingested source reports a binder campaign in which off-target panels were screened systematically, and the chapter cannot say what fraction of designed binders are cross-reactive.
- **Is the epitope a design variable?** Every method takes hotspots as input. [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]] gets closest to automating the choice by predicting high-propensity patches with MaSIF-site, and [[stark-2025-boltzgen]] generated against nine targets **without specifying any binding site** at all — but neither reports how epitope choice affects outcome, and [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] shows that within one project epitope comparisons are confounded by per-arm promotion gates. Whether some epitopes are simply undesignable, and whether that is predictable in advance, is open.
- **Did the interface prior survive the transition from hallucination to diffusion?** [[anishchenko-2021-hallucination]] attributes unintended oligomerisation to a network having learned native interface sequence signatures from the PDB. [[rfdiffusion]] is fine-tuned from a structure predictor trained on the same data, so the prior is presumably present — but whether it *helps* binder design (a useful bias toward realistic interfaces) or *hurts* it (a bias toward native-like rather than designed interfaces) is not addressed by any ingested source.
- **Was adversarial overfitting a property of the optimiser or of the missing prior?** [[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]] and [[cho-2025-protein-hunter]] both imply the latter, by searching inside a prior and succeeding where [[wicky-2022-symmetric-assembly-design]] failed. But neither runs the controlled experiment — the same objective, the same evaluation budget, with and without a prior — so the inference is architectural rather than empirical.
- **Why do the published and operated accounts of the same model disagree?** [[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]] concludes MPNN redesign is unnecessary for Complexa; [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] finds the co-designed sequence unusable on its target because of antigen copying. Candidate explanations — target hardness, the use of a search reward that copying can exploit, benchmark targets having bound homologues in training — are not distinguished by either source. This is the chapter's most consequential open question because it is the only place where a benchmark number and an operational measurement of one model can be compared at all.
- **Does success transfer off the targets the filters were tuned on?** The AF2-with-initial-guess protocol was validated on minibinder complexes including FGFR2 and IL-7Rα ([[bennett-2023-binder-design-dl-supp]]), and the field's benchmark targets recur across papers (PD-L1 appears in [[watson-2023-rfdiffusion]], [[gainza-2023-surface-fingerprint-binders]] and [[stark-2025-boltzgen]]). [[stark-2025-boltzgen]]'s 66%-versus-80% spread between novel and benchmark targets is the only quantification of the gap, from one model.
- **How far does a 256-residue training ceiling generalise?** [[lin-2026-genie3]] trains on nothing longer than 256 residues — AFDB monomers filtered to 20–256 residues, and Pinder complexes with the *whole complex* capped at 256 — then attributes its performance on 300–800-residue monomers and on multimeric design problems to out-of-domain generalisation, with an ablation showing its architectural changes "only impact designs longer than 700 residues". The claim is not new to Genie 3 — [[lin-2023-genie]] trained its long models at the same 256 maximum and [[lin-2024-genie2]] states the computational reason and claims length generalisation past it on the same grounds, so this is the third iteration of a standing house argument. It is plausible on its own evidence and untested at the sizes that matter operationally: the PfLDH system in [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] is a 632-residue target in a 711-residue complex, roughly 2.8× the largest complex Genie 3 ever saw in training. Whether equivariance is what buys the extrapolation, as that paper argues, or whether the extrapolation simply has a limit nobody has looked for, is open.
- **Was abandoning SE(3)-equivariance a mistake?** [[lin-2026-genie3]] is the only ingested source that tests the question rather than assuming an answer, and finds equivariance competitive-to-better while being faster. One paper from one group is not a settled reversal, and no source runs the matched-architecture ablation that would isolate the equivariance contribution from the branched-polymer representation, the sampling change and the training recipe.
- **Is there a shared benchmark?** No. [[butcher-2025-rfdiffusion3-supp]] defines PPI, DNA-binder, small-molecule and atomic-motif-enzyme suites; [[didi-2026-complexa-scaling]] follows a different convention with different thresholds; [[cho-2025-protein-hunter]] reports relative ipTM against RFdiffusion. This is the `benchmarks/` gap already noted in [[overview]], and binder design is where it bites hardest — three benchmark and affinity-dataset PDFs sit un-ingested in `sources/` (see §14).
- **What does an interface cost in compute, per binder that works?** [[ruijgrok-2026-binder-pipeline-comparison]] gives cost per *candidate* (8.40 to 54.60 GPU-minutes) and is explicit that this is not cost per usable lead. Combining the two requires a comparable hit rate, which §12 argues does not exist. No source in the wiki can state the compute cost of one validated binder.
