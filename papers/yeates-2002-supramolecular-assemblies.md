# Designing supramolecular protein assemblies

**A review, not original work** — but a review written by the authors of the nanohedra design it describes, which makes it a first-party account of the founding symmetric-fusion result rather than a third-party summary.

**Scope.** Surveys how biological assembly phenomena — amyloidogenesis, domain swapping and symmetric oligomerization — were inspiring strategies for designing proteins that self-assemble into supramolecular complexes, covering **filaments, symmetric cages and regular arrays**. Written in 2002, it is a snapshot of the field roughly a decade before the deep-learning era and before the Baker-lab computational nanomaterial line.

**The symmetric-fusion strategy, first-hand.** The core content this wiki relies on is the account of Padilla, Colovos & Yeates, *PNAS* 2001 ("Nanohedra: using symmetry to design self-assembling protein cages, layers, crystals, and filaments"), which the review annotates as a highlighted reference and describes in the first person:

- **The method.** Polyvalence is achieved by **genetically fusing two distinct, naturally oligomeric proteins into a single chain**; the fusion is then driven to self-assemble through the natural oligomeric interfaces of its components. A method was introduced for joining two proteins in a **geometrically defined** fashion, and the specific geometric requirements were articulated for a range of materials.
- **What determines the product.** Which assembly results depends on the **geometric relationship between the oligomeric components once fused** — the disposition of the symmetry axes each component carries. A dimeric protein fused to a trimeric protein through an **α-helical linker** gives different geometries as the linker length is varied; different symmetry-axis geometries yield different regular complexes. The review illustrates both a **two-dimensional hexagonal layer** and a **tetrahedrally symmetric cage** from the same strategy.
- **What was built.** A cage designed to be tetrahedrally symmetric, composed of **twelve 49 kDa subunits**, was demonstrated experimentally — a fusion of **bromoperoxidase (a trimer) and influenza virus matrix protein M1 (a dimer)**, shown by molecular model and transmission electron micrograph. The 2001 paper characterized two designs experimentally: a self-assembling filament and a self-assembling cage.
- **Applications anticipated.** Cage-like assemblies as molecular delivery vehicles, or as catalysts active only on substrates able to reach their interior volume.

**Significance / how the wiki uses it.** This replaces the second-hand account of the nanohedra work that [[symmetric-oligomer-design]] previously carried via [[luo-2016-protein-assembly-review]]. It does not fully close the gap — it is still a review, and the primary *PNAS* 2001 paper remains the ideal source — but it substantially upgrades it, since the authors describing the design are the authors who did it, and the geometric reasoning (symmetry-axis disposition, linker-controlled geometry, which point group results) is stated directly rather than paraphrased by a third party.

Two cautions on using it. It is **twenty-four years old**, so its assessment of what is achievable is superseded throughout — it predates the entire computational-interface-design line as well as everything deep-learning. And as a review, individual claims trace to the primary papers it cites, not to it.
